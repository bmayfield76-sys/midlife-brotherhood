# LinkedIn Version — "How to Navigate a Layoff"

*Companion post to the blog. Copy everything below the line. Put the newsletter link in the first comment.*

---

I've been laid off twice. 13 months the first time. 12 months and a week the second — I counted.

Both times started the same way. I walk into the office and the IT director and the HR guy are sitting at a table with a packet. The second time I knew what time it was the second I saw them. The Three Amigos of Layoffs.

Embarrassed. A little relieved. Then the drive home and the question that doesn't leave you alone for a year: now what?

If you're there right now, this is the version I wish somebody had handed me on day one.

---

In 2011 I paid a professional $300 to write my resume. It came back looking like a C-suite executive's. Beautiful fonts. Three pages.

Four months. Zero callbacks.

Then a recruiter called and asked me two questions. "Did you pay a service to write that?" and "Has anyone been reaching out to you?"

Yes. And no. Not one.

"Yeah. A tech manager isn't going to read all that. They want simple and direct."

We cut it to the bone. The callbacks started. I filed that away as THE lesson.

Which is why, fourteen years later, it almost sank me.

---

In 2024 I had a great severance, a part-time gig, and an unemployment check. I "casually" looked for work. I thought I had it made.

Then February 2025. Unemployment ran out. The part-time job went out of business. I failed my certification exam by one question.

I went from 5 resumes a week to 5 a day. Some weekends, 20. Simple and direct, like the man told me.

Screening calls: once in a blue moon.

Here's what nobody tells you. You are not applying to a person anymore. You're applicant 267 of 339, and software decides whether a human ever reads a word. Simple and direct was still right. It just wasn't enough.

Whatever worked for you last time — assume it's out of date.

---

What I got wrong both times: I thought I had time.

Severance in the bank. "I'll start after the holidays." The market doesn't care about your runway. Start aggressively on day one.

---

The rules that actually worked:

Take care of yourself first. The company that let you go couldn't care less about your health. So you have to.

Tell your wife first. This is a pivotal moment in your marriage. She's on your team.

Single? Stay your ass away from dating. Your aura is off.

Wake up grateful, go to sleep accomplished. Gratitude journal in the morning. 3–5 goals at night.

Network. I got my current job through a guy who knew a guy. Not a job board.

Call every creditor. Defer the loans. Find side income — DoorDash if you have to. Idleness is the enemy.

Take one day off from all of it. Forget the search exists. Just for a day.

---

On interviewing: prepare, then forget it. You'll get rejected in minutes. Ghosted for five weeks. Told you're the top candidate and then watch them hire someone else.

It's almost like dating. Don't take it personal.

---

Somewhere in that five-week ghosting I said something out loud in my kitchen: I will never again rely on income that's outside my control.

That's how Midlife Brotherhood started. Not out of desperation — out of needing something purposeful to do instead of sulking.

Go in knowing you're replaceable. So is that job.
